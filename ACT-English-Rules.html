ACT English Rules

Study Guide — memorize the rule, then the examples

Punctuation
P1Series Comma

Use commas to separate three or more items in a list. Include a comma before the final “and” or “or.”

✗She bought apples, oranges and bananas.
✓She bought apples, oranges, and bananas.
✗He is tall dark and handsome.
✓He is tall, dark, and handsome.
P2Comma + FANBOYS

Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) when it joins two independent clauses.

✗She studied hard but she still failed the test.
✓She studied hard, but she still failed the test.
✗I wanted to go yet I had too much work.
✓I wanted to go, yet I had too much work.
P3Introductory Comma

Place a comma after an introductory clause or phrase before the main clause.

✗After the storm ended the streets were flooded.
✓After the storm ended, the streets were flooded.
✗Running late for class she skipped breakfast.
✓Running late for class, she skipped breakfast.
P4Nonrestrictive Comma

Set off nonrestrictive (removable) phrases or appositives with commas on BOTH sides. If you can delete it and the sentence still makes sense, it needs commas.

✗My brother Marcus works at NASA.
✓My brother, Marcus, works at NASA.
✗The Eiffel Tower which was built in 1889 attracts millions of visitors.
✓The Eiffel Tower, which was built in 1889, attracts millions of visitors.
P5Superfluous Comma

Do not insert a comma where none is needed — especially not between a subject and verb, or within a restrictive phrase.

✗The student, who sits in the front row, always answers first. (if “who sits in the front row” is essential)
✓The student who sits in the front row always answers first.
✗She, quickly finished her homework.
✓She quickly finished her homework.
P6Semicolon

A semicolon joins two independent clauses without a conjunction. Both sides must be complete sentences.

✗I love hiking, it keeps me fit.
✓I love hiking; it keeps me fit.
✗She was exhausted; having run ten miles.
✓She was exhausted; she had run ten miles.
P7Colon

A colon follows an independent clause and introduces a list, explanation, or elaboration. The left side must be a complete sentence.

✗My favorite sports are: soccer, tennis, and swimming.
✓I have three favorite sports: soccer, tennis, and swimming.
✗She had one goal, to win the championship.
✓She had one goal: to win the championship.
P8Dash

Em dashes set off a parenthetical element with more emphasis than commas. Must come in pairs unless used at the end of a sentence.

✗The solution—lower taxes was proposed by the senator.
✓The solution—lower taxes—was proposed by the senator.
✗He finally revealed his secret, he was the anonymous donor.
✓He finally revealed his secret—he was the anonymous donor.
P9Apostrophe

Use apostrophes for contractions (it’s = it is) and possessives (the dog’s bone). Never use an apostrophe to form a plural. “Its” (possessive) has no apostrophe.

✗The dog wagged it's tail.
✓The dog wagged its tail.
✗The students' project's were displayed in the hall.
✓The students' projects were displayed in the hall.
P10End Punctuation

Choose the correct end punctuation: period for statements, question mark for direct questions. Never create a fragment.

✗She asked whether the library was open? (indirect question — no question mark)
✓She asked whether the library was open.
✗Running through the park at dawn. (fragment)
✓She was running through the park at dawn.
Sentence Structure
S1Run-on / Fused Sentence

Two independent clauses cannot be joined with no punctuation. Fix with a period, semicolon, or comma + conjunction.

✗The rain poured down we stayed inside.
✓The rain poured down, so we stayed inside.
S2Comma Splice

A comma alone cannot join two independent clauses. Fix with a semicolon, a period, or add a conjunction after the comma.

✗I finished the test early, I used the extra time to review.
✓I finished the test early, so I used the extra time to review.
S3Fragment

Every sentence needs a subject and a finite verb. Participial phrases (-ing, -ed) and dependent clauses cannot stand alone.

✗Running through the halls and shouting.
✓She was running through the halls and shouting.
✗Because the weather was terrible.
✓We canceled the picnic because the weather was terrible.
S4Subordinating Conjunction

Choose the conjunction that matches the logical relationship: “although/even though” (concession), “because/since” (cause), “while/whereas” (contrast), “if/unless” (condition).

✗Although she practiced every day, she improved rapidly.
✓Because she practiced every day, she improved rapidly.
S5Parallel Structure

Items in a list or comparison must have the same grammatical form. If one item is a verb, all must be verbs.

✗She likes hiking, to swim, and cycling.
✓She likes hiking, swimming, and cycling.
✗He is talented, hardworking, and has ambition.
✓He is talented, hardworking, and ambitious.
S6Modifier Placement

A modifier must be placed directly next to what it modifies. A dangling modifier has nothing in the sentence to attach to.

✗Walking down the street, the trees were beautiful.
✓Walking down the street, she admired the beautiful trees.
S7Faulty Comparison

Compare like things. “The population of NYC is larger than LA” is wrong — compare populations to populations, not a population to a city.

✗The rules of chess are more complex than checkers.
✓The rules of chess are more complex than those of checkers.
S8Sentence Combining

When combining sentences, choose the option that is grammatically correct, logically clear, and most concise — without changing meaning.

✗The storm was severe. It was also unexpected. It caused widespread damage.
✓The severe, unexpected storm caused widespread damage.
Agreement
A1Subject-Verb Agreement (Basic)

A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb. Ignore prepositional phrases between subject and verb.

✗The list of items are on the counter.
✓The list of items is on the counter.
✗Each of the students have a textbook.
✓Each of the students has a textbook.
A2Subject-Verb Agreement (Intervening Phrase)

Don’t be fooled by a phrase between the subject and verb. “The box of chocolates IS on the table” — the subject is “box,” not “chocolates.”

✗The quality of her paintings have improved.
✓The quality of her paintings has improved.
A3Subject-Verb Agreement (Inverted/Collective)

In inverted sentences (“There are...”), the verb agrees with the noun that follows. Collective nouns (team, group) take singular verbs.

✗There was many reasons to celebrate.
✓There were many reasons to celebrate.
✗The committee have reached a decision.
✓The committee has reached a decision.
A4Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement (Number)

A pronoun must agree in number with its antecedent. “Each” is singular — use “his or her,” not “their.”

✗Every athlete must submit their medical form.
✓Every athlete must submit his or her medical form.
A5Pronoun Reference (Ambiguous)

A pronoun must clearly refer to one specific antecedent. If “it” or “they” could refer to multiple nouns, rewrite to eliminate ambiguity.

✗When Maria met Sofia, she was nervous.
✓When Maria met Sofia, Maria was nervous.
A6Pronoun Case (Who/Whom)

Use “who” when the pronoun is a subject. Use “whom” when it is an object. Trick: substitute “he” → who; “him” → whom.

✗The scientist whom discovered penicillin changed medicine.
✓The scientist who discovered penicillin changed medicine.
✗To who should I address the letter?
✓To whom should I address the letter?
A7Verb Tense Consistency

Stay in the same tense throughout a passage unless there is a clear reason to shift. Don’t drift from past to present without cause.

✗She walked into the room and sees the surprise party.
✓She walked into the room and saw the surprise party.
A8Verb Tense Sequence (Past Perfect)

Use past perfect (“had + verb”) only when one past action clearly preceded another past action.

✗By the time we arrived, the show already started.
✓By the time we arrived, the show had already started.
A9Subjunctive Mood

Use subjunctive for hypotheticals and wishes: “If I were you...” (not “was”), “The committee recommended that he be present” (not “is”).

✗If I was taller, I would play basketball.
✓If I were taller, I would play basketball.
✗The doctor recommended that she takes the medication.
✓The doctor recommended that she take the medication.
Word Choice
W1Conciseness

The shortest correct answer is usually right. Eliminate redundancy, padding, and any phrase that adds no new information.

✗Due to the fact that it was raining, we stayed inside.
✓Because it was raining, we stayed inside.
✗She is a woman who is known for her kindness.
✓She is known for her kindness.
W2Commonly Confused Words

Know these pairs: its/it’s • their/there/they’re • affect/effect • then/than • fewer/less • lay/lie • who/whom • eminent/imminent

✗The storm will effect the entire coastline.
✓The storm will affect the entire coastline.
✗She has less friends than she did in high school.
✓She has fewer friends than she did in high school.
W3Idiomatic Preposition

Some verbs and adjectives demand specific prepositions: interested IN • responsible FOR • different FROM • capable OF • superior TO

✗She is very interested about marine biology.
✓She is very interested in marine biology.
✗His approach is different than mine.
✓His approach is different from mine.
W4Adjective vs. Adverb

Adjectives modify nouns; adverbs modify verbs, adjectives, and other adverbs. “She runs quick” is wrong — use “quickly.”

✗He spoke incredible softly.
✓He spoke incredibly softly.
✗She felt badly about the mistake.
✓She felt bad about the mistake.
W5Diction / Precision

Choose the word that is most precise AND matches the passage’s tone. Avoid words that are too informal, too vague, or have the wrong connotation.

✗The scientist’s findings were pretty important to the field.
✓The scientist’s findings were significant to the field.
✗The treaty had a big impact on relations between the two nations.
✓The treaty had a profound impact on relations between the two nations.
Rhetoric
R1Transition (Within Paragraph)

Match the transition to the logical relationship: however/but (contrast) • therefore/thus (result) • furthermore (addition) • for example (illustration)

✗She trained for months. Furthermore, she lost the race.
✓She trained for months. However, she lost the race.
R2Transition (Between Paragraphs)

A paragraph transition sentence must connect the content of the paragraph that just ended to the topic of the paragraph about to begin.

✗Starting a new paragraph with “Additionally” when the new paragraph actually contrasts the previous one.
✓Use “In contrast” or “However” to signal a shift in direction.
R3Add/Delete Sentence

When asked whether to add or delete: ask (1) does this serve the stated purpose? and (2) is it relevant to the paragraph’s focus? Both must be true to keep it.

✗Adding “Einstein also enjoyed sailing” to a paragraph about his theory of relativity.
✓Delete it — it’s true but off-topic for this paragraph’s focus.
R4Relevance

A detail is relevant only if it directly supports the paragraph’s main idea. Even interesting or true details should be deleted if they distract.

✗Including “Darwin was also an avid beetle collector” in a paragraph about natural selection.
✓Delete it — interesting, but it distracts from the paragraph’s scientific argument.
R5Ordering / Sentence Placement

The correct placement follows logically from the preceding sentence and leads into the following sentence. Look for pronoun antecedents and transitional cues.

✗Placing “It was first used in 1928” before introducing what “it” refers to.
✓First introduce the subject (penicillin), then place the date sentence after.
R6Opening / Closing

An effective opening introduces the main topic. An effective closing refers back to the essay’s central idea and wraps up the argument.

✗Opening an essay about climate change with “Throughout history, humans have faced many challenges.”
✓“Rising sea levels now threaten 40% of the world’s population.” (specific and on-topic)
R7Writer’s Goal

Read the goal statement carefully. Ask: does the passage primarily accomplish THIS specific goal? A passage can be well-written but still not meet a particular stated goal.

✗Saying a passage “provides a step-by-step guide” when it only gives a general overview.
✓Check whether the passage actually has numbered steps. If not, the goal is not met.
R8Conciseness in Context

Before deleting, ask: does removing this change the meaning or leave the sentence grammatically incomplete? If the phrase is necessary, it stays.

✗Deleting “by the river” from “The town founded by the river thrived for centuries.”
✓Keep it — removing it creates a grammatical error (“The town founded thrived” makes no sense).
